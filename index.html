PROPOSED WORKSHOP

AI Tools, Not “AI Friends”

Rethinking the personification of AI, and designing for increased user agency and reflection.

Proposed workshop
01 — OVERVIEW

Can we design AI as something other than a “friend”?

AI interfaces have largely been built around two choices: limiting interaction to chat, and marketing AI as a friend, coach, or teammate. This workshop asks whether those choices actually serve the people using these systems — or whether they quietly cost them agency, reflection, and accountability.

The development of AI interfaces has been driven mostly by tech companies and the AI community, and has converged on personifying AI: introducing it as a companion that listens, remembers, and talks back. Recent HCI research has begun documenting what this framing costs — emotional dependence on personified systems, susceptibility to affect-laden deceptive design, and a quiet shift of accountability away from developers and onto the “person” the system pretends to be.

Exhibit — marketing copy for a commercial AI wearable
“friend is a wearable companion. Worn on a necklace, always listening. Every friend is born with their own voice and personality. Talk back and forth out loud, or text privately in the friend app.”
Cited in the workshop proposal as representative of the personification trend under discussion.

At the same time, most AI products have narrowed interaction down to a chat bar or a voice icon — a small amount of real estate for a wide range of work. That narrowing can produce a “keyhole effect”: users lose track of context, are forced to verbalize things that would be easier to point at or arrange, and get less room for the kind of epistemic action that richer interfaces allow. It also runs against decades of HCI work on design friction, seamful design, slow technology, and reflective design — work that treats moments of resistance and visibility as features, not bugs, of a well-designed system.

This workshop brings the HCI community together to ask how design research that predates the current wave of AI products — on friction, reflection, seams, and pace — could be applied to it, and what alternative metaphors for AI might look like once “friend” is off the table.

02 — GUIDING QUESTIONS

What the workshop sets out to answer

Four questions structure the day and its outputs:

Q1

What design goals should we seek, as a community, for AI systems?

Q2

How do we define the role of AI in human-AI collaboration?

Q3

How could we change the AI interaction interface to meet those design goals?

Q4

Should we de-personify AI — and if so, how should we go about it?

03 — WORKSHOP DESIGN

Three activities, one shared roadmap

Activity I

Bringing in design concepts

Participants bring existing HCI concepts that are rarely applied to today's AI interfaces — design friction, seamful design, slow technology, and reflective design are starting points, not the full list. The goal is a shared set of design lenses to draw on for the rest of the day, and for future work, rather than reinventing these ideas from scratch.

Activity II

AI metaphors beyond personhood

Small groups generate alternative metaphors for AI that don't rely on framing it as a person, friend, coach, or teammate — drawing instead on tools, instruments, environments, or non-human collaborators. Groups share their metaphors with the full workshop and critique the benefits and drawbacks of designing around each one.

Activity III

Brainstorming design goals & best practices

Building on the concepts and metaphors surfaced earlier, participants collaboratively draft concrete design goals and best practices. The organizers propose prioritizing user agency, reflection, and transparency over engagement or efficiency alone — but the goal list itself is built together with participants.

04 — AFTER THE WORKSHOP

What the workshop aims to produce

Output 1

A research agenda

Focused on AI that serves the end user, rather than one optimized for engagement or business needs alone.

Output 2

Cross-institution collaboration

Connecting researchers who are independently working to redesign AI interactions and interfaces.

Output 3

Published best practices

A set of design best practices for AI systems, with interested participants invited to co-author.

As a follow-up, organizers and interested participants plan to publish a paper summarizing the workshop's outcomes, metaphors, and best practices, and to pursue a journal special issue for the submitted short papers.

05 — CALL FOR PARTICIPATION

How to apply

Applicants may submit in one of two formats. The statement-of-interest track will only open if the short-paper track does not fill the workshop.

Short paper
4 pages, excl. references · single or multi-author

Explores AI design paradigms, user perceptions of AI interfaces or interactions, or related topics. Accepted papers will be published on the workshop website.

Statement of interest
1–2 pages · single author

A brief statement of interest in the topic and reasons for joining the workshop. Opens only if the short-paper track is undersubscribed.

Accepted participants will take part in two pre-workshop activities: reviewing a shared list of design methodologies compiled from all submissions, and introducing themselves in a shared working document to help form groups around common interests.
